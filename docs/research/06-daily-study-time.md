# Research 06 — daily study time (2026-11-24)

Question: the learner wants to raise total study to **at least 1 hour a day** (pre-drill check +
app drill + night sheet combined), 6 days a week, aiming at the **July 2027 JLPT N5**. Measured so
far: 19–25 min a day on days 8–10 with the night sheet untimed (`results.md` → Time per day);
estimated 35–40 min with it. Is an hour supported, is more better, and how should it be split?

Confidence legend as `00-survey.md` (**A** meta-analysis / replications · **B** a few controlled
studies · **C** single study / review / estimate). Sources read at abstract or summary level unless
marked. Nothing here is doctrine.

## Findings

1. **Hours to N5: published estimates run from 150 to 600, and none is a study (C).**
   - The old JLPT's own specification put Level 4 at **150 hours** of study, ~100 kanji, ~800
     words (Japan's Agency for Cultural Affairs page on the test; N5 is "approximately the same
     level as the old Level 4", jlpt.jp). The post-2010 test publishes no hour figure.
   - The Japanese Language Education Center table (via Wikipedia / Wikibooks, original not
     retrieved): **325–600 hours** for N5 for learners with no kanji background, 250–450 with.
     These are language-school figures and include class time.
   - Schools and blogs quote anything between 150 and 500 (Coto Academy alone shows 180–210 on one
     page and 350–500 on another). Marketing, not measurement.
   - FSI's 2,200 class hours for Japanese is for professional working proficiency (ILR 3), not N5.
     Context only.
   → No one knows the number for a self-studying adult. Treat 150 as the floor of the estimates,
   not a promise.

2. **What an hour a day buys by the test (arithmetic, not a finding).** If the test falls on the
   first Sunday of July as in other years, that is 2027-07-04 (date not yet announced; check
   jlpt.jp in early 2027). From 2026-11-24 that is 222 days, **190 study days** at 6 a week.

   | Per study day | Total by 2027-07-04 |
   |---|---|
   | 40 min (current estimate) | ~127 h |
   | 60 min | ~190 h |
   | 90 min | ~285 h |

   40 min a day ends below every published estimate. 60 min clears the old official 150 h and
   sits under the school range. 90 min reaches the bottom of the school range.

3. **Concentrated practice has a short daily limit, shortest for beginners (B, secondhand).**
   Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer 1993 (Psychological Review), as quoted in a secondary summary
   of p. 370: no benefit from practice beyond 4 h a day, reduced benefit beyond 2 h, and the
   effective duration "may be closer to 1 hr per day"; people starting out practise for short
   periods and build up. Music and sport, not language; original page not read.
   → An hour of effortful retrieval is inside the range this literature calls productive. It gives
   no support for jumping well past it in week 2.

4. **Within one sitting, accuracy starts falling after about half an hour (B).** Randles, Harlow &
   Inzlicht 2017 (PLOS ONE, pre-registered; two samples of several thousand students using an
   online study-and-self-test program): accuracy rose slightly, then declined from about 28 minutes
   into a session, and was 4–5% below its peak by about 50 minutes. Time of day moved accuracy by
   about 1%. One program, undergraduates, multiple-choice and recall items.
   → **Split the hour.** Blocks under ~30 min. The routine already has this shape (check + drill,
   then the night sheet). Clock time of day barely matters for accuracy; `research/05` covers sleep.

5. **Spacing across sessions is the supported lever; no study sets a daily dose (A for spacing).**
   Kim & Webb 2022 (meta-analysis, 98 effect sizes, 48 experiments, N = 3,411): spaced practice
   beats massed for L2 learning. Serrano 2022 (state-of-the-art review, 47 studies): the best gap
   between sessions is still unclear; at program level some studies favour intensive courses, and
   the adult evidence is inconclusive (Serrano & Muñoz 2007's abstract favours concentrated hours).
   The review evaluates no "minutes per day" variable.
   → The literature supports *spread it out and come back*, which the ladder and the three daily
   blocks already do. It does not say 60 is better than 45 or worse than 90.

6. **Minutes are a weak predictor; what fills them is a strong one (B).**
   - Plant, Ericsson, Hill & Asberg 2005: amount of study predicted college grades only once study
     quality and prior performance were accounted for.
   - Macnamara et al. 2014 (meta-analysis, as reported): accumulated practice explained about 12%
     of performance differences overall.
   - Word-card research synthesis (Frontiers in Psychology 2022): time spent on cards correlated
     with vocabulary outcomes, not significantly.
   - Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara 2020 (meta-analysis of intentional vocabulary activities): gains
     of 60.1% (meaning recall) and 58.5% (form recall) right after study fell to 39.4% and 25.1%
     on delayed tests.
   → Extra minutes spent on more of the same thing are the weakest use. Extra minutes on review of
   what is fading, or on a skill with zero training (hearing words: `research/03`), are the
   strongest. Form recall, the app's task, decays fastest.

7. **Sleep outranks minutes (A, from `research/05` finding 5).** Restricted sleep hurts new
   learning. A longer night block that cuts sleep costs more than it adds.

## What this means for Kana Hero

- **60 minutes a day is defensible** on two grounds: it is the only pace of the three that clears
  a published N5 estimate by July (findings 1–2), and it is within the daily amount the practice
  literature treats as productive (finding 3). Neither ground is strong evidence. It is a budget,
  set by arithmetic, to be judged on the learner's own numbers.
- **Not a quota.** Finding 6: time alone predicts little. A day whose work is done in 45 minutes
  is done. No padding, no make-up minutes (treadmill guard; same rule as rest days).
- **Three blocks, each under ~30 min** (finding 4): check + drill · a midday or afternoon block ·
  the night sheet. If one block must grow, it is not the night one (finding 7).
- **Vocabulary pace vs the date (arithmetic).** 83 words introduced by day 10; ~800 is the
  unofficial N5 figure. 717 more words over 190 study days is 3.8 a study day, or 4.7 if the last
  new word lands about six weeks before the test. The current scheduler allows roughly 5
  (`results.md`, day 8; itself an estimate). **July works at the present pace with almost no
  slack.** This corrects the agent's remark of 2026-11-24 that July "needs a faster pace". The
  app's list is 210 words; the list past 210 does not exist yet and needs its own research pass.
- **Order of what fills the added minutes** (to be decided, one at a time, each with a falsifier):
  1. the load already arriving (the check and the pile both grow as words accumulate);
  2. hearing known words (`research/03`), once audio exists;
  3. more new words, only while next-day first checks hold.

## Falsifiers for a 60-minute day
- Accuracy in the later part of a block drops against the early part (ledger `ok` by position,
  check score by item order) → shorter blocks, not fewer minutes.
- Next-day first check of new words under ~60% for a week → the added load is crowding encoding.
- Sleep shortened on study nights (learner's report) → cut the night block first.
- Minutes up for four weeks with the sealed checks (2026-12-21) and the next mock flat → the extra
  time is going to the wrong activity; change what fills it before adding more.

## What this pass looked for and did not find
- Any controlled study of **minutes per day** for adult self-study of a second language. The dose
  literature is about spacing and course intensity, not daily duration.
- Anything on daily load for **handwriting** production, or for Japanese specifically.
- The original Japanese Language Education Center table and the old Test Content Specifications
  (both secondhand here).
- Full text of Ericsson 1993 p. 370, Plant 2005, Macnamara 2014, Kim & Webb 2022.
- An announced date for the July 2027 test.
