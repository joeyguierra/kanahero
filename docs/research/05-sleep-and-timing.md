# Research 05 — sleep and study timing (2026-11-23)

Question: the nightly exercise was decided "last thing before sleep" on Mazza et al. 2016, plus an
unnamed "earlier study, no 12 h day-vs-night difference" (`decisions.md` 2026-11-23, marked
"needs: research/"). Verify both, and say what timing evidence means for a learner whose pre-drill
check and drill happen any time during the day and whose nightly exercise is "evening", not strictly
last thing (learner, 2026-11-23).

Confidence legend as `00-survey.md`. Abstract/summary level unless marked.

## Findings

1. **Mazza et al. 2016 — verified, single study (B−).** Psychological Science, doi
   10.1177/0956797616659930. 40 French adults learned 16 Swahili–French word pairs, then relearned
   them 12 h later, with or without a night of sleep in between. The sleep group needed less
   relearning and recalled more at one week and at six months. Small n, one word list, one lab.
   A follow-up testing whether it generalises to other relearning methods exists (HAL hal-02487151);
   its results were not retrieved (page blocked). → The "learn, sleep, relearn" idea is real but
   rests on one study.

2. **The "earlier study, no 12 h difference" — not found.** No source located. Removed from the
   decision. Related and real: 12 h sleep-vs-wake designs cannot separate "sleep helps" from "a day
   awake hurts"; a 24 h design found memory better when sleep came shortly after learning than after
   a full day awake (PLOS ONE 2012, doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0033079).

3. **Sleep soon after learning helps vocabulary (B).** Gais, Lucas & Born 2006 (Learning & Memory):
   high-school students learning English–German vocabulary remembered more when sleep came within a
   few hours of learning than after >14 h awake, with time-of-day controlled. Berres & Erdfelder 2021
   (meta-analysis, episodic memory): a robust sleep benefit of moderate size, moderated by how memory
   is tested.

4. **Smaller and less robust than once claimed (B).** Cordi & Rasch 2021 (review): several key sleep
   and memory findings did not replicate or held only under certain conditions; effects look smaller,
   more task-dependent and less long-lasting than assumed. Learning & Memory 2023 ("Better late than
   never"): sleep still helps memories learned after long periods awake.
   → Sleep after study helps; the *exact* gap between study and sleep matters less than the early
   papers suggested.

5. **Not sleeping enough costs more than timing gains (A).** Meta-analyses: sleep restriction
   (3–6.5 h vs 7–11 h) hurts forming new memories, g = 0.29 (125 effect sizes); sleep deprivation
   before learning, g ≈ 0.62 (Newbury et al. 2021, Psych Bulletin).

## What this means for Kana Hero

- **Evening exercise: supported.** Study within a few hours of sleep is the condition with the best
  evidence (finding 3). "Strictly last thing before sleep" is not required (finding 4). The name
  "nightly / nighttime exercise" fits.
- **Morning cold item: keep.** It is the "relearn after sleep" half of Mazza's design (finding 1)
  and the after-sleep measurement.
- **Daytime check and drill: fine as they are.** Their main drivers are retrieval and spacing
  (`research/00`, `02`), which do not depend on clock time; that night's sleep still helps them
  (finding 4).
- **Never trade sleep for study** (finding 5). A late night cut short is worse than a skipped
  exercise. Consistent with "no make-up nights".
- Not a feature: no reminders, no bedtime nudges (treadmill guard).

## What would change this
- If the morning item is no better on evening-exercise nights than on nights the exercise ran
  earlier in the day, the timing is not doing visible work for this learner; keep the exercise,
  drop the timing claim. (Needs the time of each nightly exercise logged in `results.md`.)
